Congress’ nuclear reactor push alarms locals

A committee vote in Washington last week quietly moved the United States closer to deploying nuclear reactors in the Western Pacific, and for many on Guam, the implications are deeply unsettling.
The House Armed Services Committee approved an amendment to the fiscal year 2027 National Defense Authorization Act that would establish a pilot program allowing the Department of Defense to evaluate and deploy small modular reactors within the Indo-Pacific Command area of responsibility. Guam is not mentioned by name, but the amendment specifically prioritizes the Western Pacific, leaving little ambiguity about where planners are focused.
The Pacific Center for Island Security, a Guam-based think tank, sounded the alarm in a press release warning of a troubling trajectory for the island.
"The trendline for Guam is not good," said Underwood, PCIS chair. "There are many layers of concerns about placing nuclear reactors in areas where the military anticipates contested operations, and all of these apply to any plans to experiment, evaluate, or operate transportable nuclear reactors in Guam."
PCIS director Leland Bettis, in an interview Monday with The Guam Daily Post, noted that the legislation redefines what qualifies as a microreactor, expanding the term from its traditional range of one to five megawatts to anything under 50 megawatts. He said the old Piti power plant produced roughly 35 megawatts, making a reactor at the upper end of this new definition something far more consequential than the name implies.
"This is not what people would call a micro-reactor," Bettis said. "Anything up to 50 megawatts is not a micro-reactor. That's typically called a small modular reactor."
A reactor dedicated solely to military use, he said, becomes an obvious target in any conflict. And if struck by a conventional weapon, the radiological damage would extend well beyond what previous modeling for smaller units envisioned.
"I don't know what the impact of the spread is if a 50 megawatt small modular reactor was hit by a conventional weapon," Bettis said. "But it'd be significantly greater than one mile. I can guarantee you that."
Bettis also pointed out that the same day HASC adopted the amendment, the U.S. Department of Energy announced that the Antares advanced reactor had attained criticality, meaning it achieved a self-sustaining nuclear chain reaction, a key developmental milestone. Antares is the name of an advanced small modular reactor (SMR) design studied under DOE’s Reactor Pilot Program; reaching criticality is an early step in proving a design, but it does not mean the reactor is fully tested, certified as safe, or ready for deployment.
Bettis said the timing reflects a push shaped more by political deadlines than scientific readiness, noting that the Trump administration has set a target of demonstrating new reactor viability before July 4, 2026. Even after a design reaches criticality, he said, the path to full testing, independent safety review, and operational deployment usually takes years.
"The combination of expediting new nuclear reactors and the possibility of them experimenting in Guam is just not a good trend line for us," he said.
Del. James Moylan voted against the amendment. His office said he and his staff were aware of the proposal once it was filed and that his opposition was made clear throughout the markup process. His office also pointed to language he secured in the FY 2026 NDAA, now law, requiring the Secretary of Defense to give Congress and the Guam governor at least one year's notice before placing any nuclear reactor on the island.
PCIS, however, noted that only Rep. Jill Tokuda of Hawaii spoke against the amendment during the debate, explicitly naming Guam as a potential deployment site.
"We are glad that Delegate Moylan voted no on the amendment but must note that only Rep. Tokuda of Hawaii spoke out in Guam's defense," Underwood said. "Delegate Moylan was silent in the discussion. He has been likely briefed on this matter. He should share the information with the people of Guam."
Congressional candidate Mary Camacho Torres, a former senator challenging Moylan for the delegate seat, called the markup a missed opportunity.
"Representation is not just casting a vote," Torres said. "It is using every possible moment to advocate for our island. Our delegate had a chance to make that position clear. Instead, he chose silence."
Acting Gov. Josh Tenorio said in a phone interview Tuesday morning and through a formal written response that the technology has not met the bar the community deserves.
"I am not convinced that this technology has been proven or demonstrated to address safety concerns that our people have," Tenorio told the Post. "On top of that, I think that we are very hypersensitive about the use of this kind of technology because of the nuclear legacy of the Pacific."
He said the military's energy focus should be on investments already discussed, including burying transmission lines and building out the Liquid Natural Gas infrastructure connected to Guam's recently opened power plant.
"We already have enough burdens on our shoulders," Tenorio said. "We don't need additional ones."
The administration's formal response pressed for answers on where a reactor would be sited, what safety standards would govern it, and how emergencies would be handled during a typhoon, cyberattack, or conflict.
"Our position is straightforward: transparency must come first," the administration said. "The government of Guam and the people of Guam deserve a full and honest conversation before any decisions are made. For Guam, these discussions carry added significance because decisions of this magnitude can advance through Congress even though the people most affected do not have a vote on the House floor. That reality makes consultation not merely good policy but a democratic obligation."
Sen. Jesse Lujan, who chairs the committee overseeing utilities and federal and foreign affairs, called for a public briefing involving Moylan, Joint Region Marianas, Guam Power Authority, and other stakeholders before the proposal advances further.
"Any proposal involving nuclear technology on or near Guam deserves a serious, transparent, and informed conversation with the people of Guam, not just a Pentagon-driven announcement or a federal policy discussion that happens around us instead of with us," Lujan told the Post.
Sen. Therese Terlaje aligned with PCIS's overall assessment and called the situation urgent, saying local leaders must stand together in insisting on community consent.
Sen. Sabina Perez tied the proposal to Guam's existing health burdens from prior military contamination, saying it is unconscionable to add to them. She called on Congress to redirect funds toward GPA's infrastructure needs, including Mawar-related FEMA reimbursements and grid hardening.
Sen. William Parkinson said he does not oppose nuclear energy as a concept but drew a firm line at using Guam as a test site.
"The question is whether Guam should be treated as a proving ground for untested military technology while being denied any meaningful voting representation or the respect to say yes, no, or not yet until these very important questions about our aquifer, our environment, and our safety are answered," Parkinson told the Post.
He said Moylan's limited voting power makes the situation more, not less, concerning.
"A non-voting delegate is not equal representation," Parkinson said. "It is a reminder that when our vote would matter most, it does not exist."
The full NDAA has not been finalized in the House, and the Senate process remains ahead. The administration said it intends to stay engaged: “Guam will use every opportunity to influence this language or steer its administrative implementation going forward.”
