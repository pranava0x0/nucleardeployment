After 6 months in Huntsville our Electrically-Heated Demonstration Unit (EDU) campaign is complete — and we’re sharing a look inside the work that got us here.
We built the EDU because we believe you have to build a reactor to design a reactor. This unit is a full-scale, electrically heated stand-in for our core, built to push each heat pipe, interface, and material to real operating temperatures and pressures.
Over the last several months, the team has run repeated startup and steady-state cycles, pulled the hardware down between tests, inspected every surface, and fed each lesson back into our models. That tight loop — design, build, test, refine — is already shaping the first-of-a-kind reactor we’re bringing to life.
We use heat pipes to move heat from the core to our heat exchanger. They’re passive, simple, and reliable, which means we can iterate quickly without relying on exotic materials or complex pump systems that slow development.
This is the first in a series of full-scale prototypes that will take us to a clear goal: achieving criticality on American soil by July 4, 2026, and delivering our first electricity producing demonstration in 2027.

The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) has formally approved our Preliminary Documented Safety Analysis (PDSA) for the Mark-0 reactor—our first demonstration reactor, scheduled to go live before July 4, 2026. Mark-0 will validate fueling operations, reactor controls, and core physics.
This demonstration is a critical step toward generating electricity from advanced microreactors. Mark-0 uses a full-scale core and the same facility and fuel that will support our next reactor test in 2027.
The PDSA defines the preliminary safety basis for the reactor, facility, and planned operations, demonstrating that our approach meets DOE expectations at this stage. This approval validates our safety case and establishes a clear pathway to final acceptance as we prepare for fabrication, assembly, and installation.
Mark-0 will be tested at Idaho National Laboratory in Building 793—now the Reactors and Critical Experiments facility—a site with deep nuclear history. Decades ago, this building housed ML-1, the Army’s first mobile nuclear reactor. Today, it supports the next generation of deployable nuclear power. We find the historical relevance fitting and inspiring, as Antares develops a similar-scale microreactor to meet Army operational and installation needs.
Since 2024, we’ve worked to establish this facility as an enduring testbed, enabling rapid progress without the need for groundbreaking construction. We’re grateful for our partners at DOE and Idaho National Laboratory, and for leaders like Congressman Mike Simpson who continue to support the American nuclear renaissance.

Later this year, our Mark-0 reactor will go critical—achieving a self-sustaining nuclear chain reaction for the first time.
We’ll do this under the Department of Energy’s "Authorization Pathway for Nuclear Facilities" (DOE-STD-1271-2025), validating that our facility, full-scale reactor core, TRISO fuel, and control systems operate safely and as designed.
Critical operations in a full-scale reactor let us perform real fueling operations, validate neutronics models, measure reactivity and shutdown margins, and demonstrate safe, reliable control. This is where analysis meets hardware.
The milestone isn’t “going critical” for its own sake. We are fissioning uranium in a reactor we designed and built to prove we're ready to start building our first full-power reactor.
Every data point from Mark-0 reduces uncertainty and puts us one bit closer to generating power and moving electrons for customers in 2027.
This is how advanced reactors are built: step by step, with discipline, closing the gap between simulation and operation.
